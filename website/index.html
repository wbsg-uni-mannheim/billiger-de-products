Billiger.de Products: A Bilingual Entity Matching Benchmark

Aaron Steiner, Ksenia Elagin, Ralph Peeters, Christian Bizer
Data and Web Science Group, University of Mannheim
Paper (coming soon) Download Data Code

Abstract

Billiger.de Products is an entity matching benchmark built from the manually grouped product catalog of the price comparison platform billiger.de (operated by solute GmbH). To the best of our knowledge, it is the largest entity matching benchmark for German-language product data to date. The benchmark follows the multi-dimensional design of WDC Products and varies the size of the training set, the fraction of hard corner cases, and the fraction of entities unseen during training. Every record additionally exists in a machine-translated English version with identical splits, labels, and identifiers, so the effect of the data language on matching performance can be measured down to individual record pairs. Baseline results cover six supervised matchers as well as GPT-5.2 in a zero-shot setting on both language versions. The influence of the language turns out to be architecture-dependent: matchers relying on English pre-training or token overlap score higher on the English version, matchers with strong task-specific supervision score equal or higher on the German original, and GPT-5.2 differs by less than 4 F1 points between the languages.

Introduction

Entity matching is the task of deciding whether two records refer to the same real-world entity, for example whether two product offers describe the same product. The benchmarks that are used to evaluate entity matching systems, such as Abt-Buy, Amazon-Google, or WDC Products, are single-language by nature: each of them contains records in one language, almost always English. Many applications do not fit this picture. A shopper in Europe who is looking for the cheapest offer for a product does not care whether that offer is published on a German, French, or Italian shop, and a price comparison portal that wants to answer such queries has to match offers across languages. Benchmarks for evaluating matchers under these conditions barely exist.

Billiger.de Products is a step in this direction. The benchmark is built from the manually grouped product catalog of the German price comparison platform billiger.de (operated by solute GmbH) and is, to the best of our knowledge, the largest entity matching benchmark for German-language product data to date. In addition to the German original, we provide a machine-translated English version of every record. Splits, labels, and identifiers are identical in both versions, so the benchmark can be used as a German benchmark, as an English benchmark, or as a pair of parallel versions for measuring how the data language affects matching performance down to individual record pairs. Since every German record can also be paired with the English translation of its counterpart, the benchmark is also a starting point for cross-lingual matching experiments.

The benchmark follows the multi-dimensional design of WDC Products and controls task difficulty along three dimensions: the size of the training set, the fraction of hard corner cases, and the fraction of entities unseen during training. The sections below describe the benchmark in detail and report baseline results for six supervised matchers and for GPT-5.2 in a zero-shot setting on both language versions.

The Benchmark

The benchmark is derived from a snapshot of the operational matching process of billiger.de: 6,309,224 product offers grouped into 692,495 product entities, where each group was created and manually verified by platform employees. The benchmark itself contains 13,730 product offers describing 2,168 distinct products per language version. Every record carries the attributes name, description, brand, and price, with name and price complete and brand and description above 99 % coverage. Identifiers such as EAN or manufacturer part numbers are deliberately excluded so that matching has to rely on the textual description of a product rather than identifier lookup.

Following WDC Products, task difficulty is controlled along three dimensions, resulting in 9 training sets, 27 validation sets, and 9 test sets per language:

  • Training set size: small (2,500 pairs), medium (6,000 pairs), and large (26,888–27,393 pairs) training sets. The smaller sets are subsets of the larger ones, and all three are built from the same 500 product groups.
  • Corner cases: groups of four highly similar but distinct products generate hard positives and hard negatives. Datasets contain 20 %, 50 %, or 80 % corner-case product groups.
  • Unseen entities: in the Seen, Half-Seen, and Unseen test sets, 0 %, 50 %, or 100 % of the evaluated products do not occur in the training data.
  • Two languages: every record exists in the German original and in an English machine translation. Splits, labels, and identifiers are identical, so performance differences are attributable to the language of the records and the translation process.

Product domains

The products span thirteen top-level domains. Since the retailer-provided category paths follow no common standard, each product was assigned to the taxonomy through a rule-based keyword mapping over the normalized category paths, with the remaining cases classified by GPT-5-mini. A manual validation of 500 random products found 89.0 % of the assignments correct, 7.4 % ambiguous, and 3.6 % [@Chris wollen wir das so transparent sagen?]. Clothing, furniture, and electronics account for the largest shares, followed by home-related goods and electronics.

Pairs and labels

Match labels are inherited from the manually verified product groups of billiger.de. Positive pairs are generated exhaustively within each product group, negative pairs combine similarity-based hard negatives with one random negative per offer. The number of hard negatives per offer grows with the training set size.

The test sets contain roughly 4,470 pairs each with a class imbalance of 1:10 to 1:12 between matches and non-matches, the condition matchers face in production candidate streams. All nine test sets went through a two-round manual audit against a strict product identity definition: product variants that differ in size, color, configuration, capacity, or package quantity count as non-matches. The audit mainly relabelled pairs in which distinct product variants had been grouped together.

Baseline Results

Six supervised matchers spanning four methodological generations (word co-occurrence, feature-based machine learning, fine-tuned transformers, contrastive and graph-based architectures) were evaluated across all 27 configurations of both language versions, plus GPT-5.2 in a strict zero-shot setting. The three difficulty dimensions work as intended. F1 increases with the training set size, decreases as the corner-case ratio grows from 20 % to 80 %, and decreases from the Seen to the Unseen test sets. Between its easiest and its hardest German variant, each supervised model loses between 24 (Magellan) and 61 (RoBERTa) F1 points. The Seen-to-Unseen drops are larger than those reported on WDC Products, because unseen entities can come from any of the thirteen product domains instead of staying within a single familiar one.

Ditto and HierGAT are the strongest supervised systems and reach 91.76 and 89.41 F1 on the easiest German variant. Zero-shot GPT-5.2 behaves differently. Under the rule-guided prompt it varies by at most 7 F1 points across all difficulty settings (19 under the simple prompt), and it leads on every Unseen test set, where supervised models lose the training signal they depend on. Supervised matchers overtake GPT-5.2 only on Seen test sets with medium or large training data. One caveat applies to the GPT-5.2 numbers: the model also served as the flagging tool during test set verification, so the exact margins between GPT-5.2 and the supervised systems should be read with care.

Matching difficulty also differs between product domains, and the ranking is the same for every model and both languages. Tools and electronics are the easiest categories, as their product titles contain model numbers and technical specifications. Clothing and furniture are the hardest, because offers for different product variants share nearly identical descriptions. Evaluations on electronics-centric benchmarks therefore overestimate matching performance on broad product assortments.

German vs. English

Because the English version is a record-level translation of the German original with identical splits and labels, the benchmark measures the combined effect of the data language and the translation on matching performance. For most architectures this effect is small (mean differences within ±1.6 F1). Where it is larger, the direction depends on the architecture.

Matchers that depend on English surface forms score higher on the English version. WordCooc gains up to 6.5 F1 points, because German compounds and rich morphology reduce the exact token overlap it relies on. RoBERTa, pre-trained mostly on English text, wins 22 of 27 configurations on the English version with a mean difference of +2.31 F1. Magellan leans the other way, as its character-level similarity features turn the long shared substrings inside German compounds into a usable signal.

Task-specific supervision compensates the English pre-training bias. R-SupCon is balanced with 13 wins for English and 14 for German. HierGAT favours the German original in 17 of 27 configurations, including all nine large-training configurations shown above, and Ditto in 23 of 27 with a mean difference of −5.73 F1. Zero-shot GPT-5.2 differs by less than 4 F1 points between the languages in every configuration.

The practical implication is direct. Matchers that rely on English pre-trained representations or surface token statistics should not be applied to German product data without prior evaluation. Matchers with strong task-specific supervision and zero-shot LLMs transfer across the two languages without a systematic penalty.

How to Use the Benchmark

The benchmark is distributed as gzipped JSON files with one record pair per line. Each line contains both product records, the binary match label, and a hard-negative indicator. The file name encodes the configuration: products80cc20rnd050un_train_large.json.gz denotes 80 % corner cases, 20 % random pairs, 50 % unseen entities, large training split. The files can be read directly with pandas: 

import pandas as pd

pairs = pd.read_json(
    "products80cc20rnd050un_train_large.json.gz",
    compression="gzip",
    lines=True,
)

For supervised matchers, pick a training set, select hyperparameters on the corresponding validation set, and report F1 on the nine test sets. The test sets are the same for all training set sizes, so results obtained with different amounts of training data remain comparable. Please do not use the test pairs for training, hyperparameter selection, or prompt engineering.

For zero-shot and in-context learning, the nine test sets can be used directly. If a method needs demonstration examples, they should be drawn from the training or validation sets.

For language comparisons, run the same configuration on the German and the English version. Splits, labels, and identifiers are identical, so any difference in the scores is attributable to the language of the records and the translation process. Since the record identifiers line up across the two versions, German records can also be paired with English ones to build cross-lingual matching tasks.

Conclusion

Billiger.de Products extends entity matching evaluation beyond English. It combines the largest German-language product matching benchmark to date with a structurally identical English translation, covers thirteen product domains instead of a single one, and controls task difficulty along the three dimensions of WDC Products. The baseline experiments show that all three dimensions work as intended, that broad product assortments are harder to match than the electronics-centric data of most existing benchmarks, and that the effect of the data language depends on the architecture of the matcher. A matcher that was only evaluated on English data should therefore not be expected to behave the same on German offers.
